# What Should a Court Reporter Charge? Setting Rates That Survive an Audit

Freelance court reporters ask the rate question the same way every year. Not "what does everyone charge," but "is mine defensible?" An appearance fee you undercharge is money you gave away. A per-page rate nobody can justify falls apart the moment a firm pushes back, or the moment you realize what the work actually costs you.

There is no state-by-state rate card in most places, and the freelancers who bill well aren't guessing. They know the parts of the job that cost money and they price each one. Here's the same math they run, in the order it matters.

## Start with the appearance fee, because that's your floor

Every deposition has a cost just for showing up: the drive, the setup, the waiting, the parking. That's the appearance fee, and it's the floor under everything else. Every rate you quote sits on it.

**Half-day and full-day rates are the norm.** Survey data and agency rate cards from 2026 cluster around $275 to $475 for a half day and $475 to $650 for a full day, with hearings and trial days running higher than depos. If your full-day number sits below your half-day number times two, you're discounting the tired hours, which is backwards.

Two questions sharpen it, and both are worth deciding before the next deposition lands.

- **How long is "half"?** Four hours is common. If you set three, you'll eat the gap on every late-running morning.
- **What happens on cancels?** A same-day cancellation burns a day you couldn't sell. Some reporters bill a partial appearance fee on late cancels and say so up front. Firms respect the ones who write it down.

## Per-page rates carry the real business

The transcript pages are where the money compounds. One 200-page deposition at a weak rate loses more than a bad appearance week ever will. The rate you set for pages is a career decision wearing a small number.

Current ranges hold steady around **$4.50 to $5.50 per page for standard turnaround** on ordinary depos, with rough drafts and rush jobs moving up from there. Three things move you inside that range, and you should know which ones apply to you:

- **Turnaround.** Standard is cheaper than expedite, always. Page-count work makes this obvious: the same transcript priced two ways, days apart.
- **Realtime.** Streaming your draft live to counsel adds risk and prep. Realtime jobs bill meaningfully higher per page, and the reporters doing them are rarely the cheapest ones in the book.
- **Weight class.** Medical terminology, expert testimony, multiple speakers, four-volume document dumps. Heavy work costs more to produce, and the per-page rate should say so.

A useful self-check is to take your last ten invoices and divide earnings by pages produced. If that blended number embarrasses you, the rate card is the place to fix it, not the hours.

## The add-ons you're forgetting to bill

This is where freelance reporters quietly leak income. The charges are small, the paperwork is annoying, and nobody sends a reminder. Over a year it's the difference between a good quarter and a great one.

**Mileage.** Travel to depos and courthouses adds up fast. The IRS business rate was 72.5 cents a mile through June 2026, rising to 76 cents from July 1. A 60-mile round trip is $43.50 at the first-half rate. If you drive it weekly and never bill travel, that's real money left on the table. (I ran the numbers after a reporter told me she'd been eating her mileage for a year, and the total made her wince.)

**Expedites.** Rush turnaround fees commonly run 25 to 100 percent on top of the base page rate, or flat hourly charges for same-day work. Write your expedite rules down and bill them without flinching. A client who asks for overnight and complains about the fee was never your client.

**Rough drafts.** A working first copy is a product. It takes formatting passes and disclaimers. Bill it. And don't apologize for it, because a rough draft is a product.

**Copies and the small stuff.** Additional counsel copies, postage, the certification of the original you sometimes get asked for. Every one of these costs you actual dollars or actual minutes, and clients rarely notice their absence on the invoice. They always notice when another reporter bills them, which tells you the market accepts them.

**Scopist and proofing help.** If you pay a scopist to get pages out, that's part of your cost of goods. Your page rate needs to cover the help you buy, or the help comes out of your pocket.

## Then test the whole card against your year

Rates are a system, not a list of numbers. The parts can look fine and the system still wobble, which is why the last step is a whole-year test, not a line-item review. Once the parts look sane, check the assembled machine against reality this way,

1. Pull your calendar from the last quarter and count working days.
2. Divide total transcript income by pages delivered for the blended page rate.
3. Compare mileage and other add-ons actually billed against what you actually drove and did.
4. Look at your slow weeks. Were they price problems or pipeline problems? Those need different fixes, and confusing them sends people chasing rates when the real issue was outreach.

If your blended page rate sits near the bottom of the range while your calendar runs full, you aren't underpricing, you're busy at the wrong margin. If the calendar has holes, price may matter less than how you're marketing. Both are diagnostic, and both are fixable.

## Keep the card where it can't drift (and where it's memory stays honest)

A rate card that lives in your head changes every time you're tired, and your invoicing inherits the drift. Court reporters who bill consistently write the numbers out somewhere real. Appearance fees, page rates by turnaround, realtime premiums, expedite percentages, mileage, and yes, the small stuff too.
