The Court Reporter Invoice, Line by Line

Every line item a freelance court reporter should bill: appearance fees, per-page transcript charges, copies, rough drafts, exhibits, scopist deductions, mileage, and expedites, plus what a complete invoice includes.

By Jeff Kohler, founder of StenoDesk · October 2026

An invoice from a freelance court reporter can run seven lines long, and every line has a story. Firms see hundreds of these. They notice when a reporter's invoice reads like it was built by someone who bills depos for a living, and they notice even more when it doesn't.

The difference is rarely honesty. It's completeness. A vague invoice gets questions, and questions delay payment. An invoice with every line item spelled out gets paid the way the transcript does, on time and without a second email.

The line items that belong on almost every invoice

Appearance fee. The charge for showing up, whether that's a depos office downtown or a video conference from your spare room. Current 2026 rate cards put half days around $275 to $475 and full days at $475 to $650, with trial days higher. If your job ran long, say so on the invoice, like "appearance, full day (6 hrs)" beats a bare number every time.

Original transcript pages. The heart of the invoice. Standard-turnaround work on ordinary depos typically runs $4.50 to $5.50 per page. List the page count and the rate separately. "Transcript, 212 pages × $5.25" says exactly what it delivered, where a single merged number invites a question. Realtime work belongs on its own line at its own rate.

Copies. Additional counsel copies bill per page or per copy, depending on how you've set your card. Cheap to forget, easy to bill when the line's already on the page.

Rough draft. If any party got a working draft before the certified original, that draft is a billable product. Format passes and disclaimers take real time. Bill the pages or the flat fee you decided on, and note it's a draft.

Exhibits. Numbered exhibits cost minutes to handle and pages to scan. A flat per-exhibit charge is the common approach. When a depo runs exhibit-heavy, this line quietly becomes one of the larger numbers on the invoice.

Scopist or proofing deduction. If you're paying a scopist or proofreader out of the job's proceeds, show it. Seeing the deduction on the firm-facing invoice is normal in this trade and it explains a lower net without any awkward follow-up call.

Mileage. Travel to the job. The IRS business standard was 72.5 cents a mile for the first half of 2026 and 76 cents from July 1. Round trips add up over a month of depos. Put the miles and the math on the invoice line, because a bare two-digit number invites exactly the question you don't want.

Expedites and convenience charges. Overnight or same-day turnaround commonly runs 25 to 100 percent over your base page rate. Some reporters bill it as a percentage, some as a flat fee. Either is fine. What isn't fine by the client's accounting department is an unexplained rush charge, so write what it's for.

Shipping and postage. Certified originals and exhibits still move by courier more often than anyone expects. Rebill the actual cost, or round to the dollar if that's your house style.

What a complete invoice has besides line items

  • Your business identity. Name, address, email, phone. The people paying you have to be able to reach the person who sent the bill (and yes, that means your real phone number, not a fax line from 2009).
  • The client's matter. Case name, caption, or matter number. the deposition date. the parties. This lets their bookkeeper match your invoice to a file instead of asking.
  • Invoice number and date. Sequential, dated on send day. If your billing runs behind a week, the invoice date is where firms start judging payment terms from, so send them promptly.
  • Line items and a grand total. Obvious, and skipped constantly anyway.
  • Payment terms in plain words. "Net 30" or "due on receipt", whichever you actually enforce. Firms will follow whatever the invoice says, including nothing, so say something.
  • How to pay. A check address, a payment link, or card instructions. Remove every excuse to pay late.

The mistakes that show up on real invoices

The same handful come up again and again. Merged totals with no line items behind them, which reads as a number to negotiate rather than a bill to pay. Vanished mileage, the reporter drove, the invoice forgot. Rush fees with no explanation attached, which is how a $90 expedite becomes a 40-minute dispute. Terms blank or vague. And the invoice that lands two weeks after the transcript shipped, sitting in a queue behind fresher bills.

Every one of these costs the same reporter money twice. Once in the billing coversation, and once again in the delay.

How the good ones do it

Rate card written down. Line items remembered per client, because one firm's "net 30" is another's "due on receipt." Charges pulled from the job itself, miles driven, exhibits logged, pages counted, rather than from recall. The invoice out the door the same week the transcript ships.
