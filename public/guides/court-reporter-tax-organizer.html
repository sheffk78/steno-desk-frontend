The 1099 Organizer for Court Reporters

A yearly tax organizer for self-employed court reporters: 1099 income tracking, scopist deductions, mileage logs, equipment and Section 179, software subscriptions, dues, and a quarterly routine that keeps April calm.

By Jeff Kohler, founder of StenoDesk · October 2026

Freelance court reporting is a Schedule C business whether you treat it like one or not. The 1099s and the checks arrive, the dues and the equipment receipts pile up somewhere, and April does what April does. The reporters who file without drama aren't more organized by personality. They keep a short list of categories current all year, and the list is smaller than you'd expect.

Here's the whole picture. It's built from what the IRS form actually asks, and what court reporting actually spends.

The income side

Money comes in as 1099-NECs from firms and agencies, plain checks from attorneys, and card payments through a payment link. All of it is business income. The mistake that costs real money is forgetting the checks that never generated a 1099, a 1099 threshold means some clients won't file one. The income is taxable either way. Keep a running total somewhere that isn't a shoebox.

If you billed a firm and paid a scopist out of the same job, both numbers matter separately on your return. Gross income and cost of goods aren't the same line, and a lot of freelance reporters overstate one because they conflated them all year.

The deductions that matter for court reporters

Scopists and proofreaders. Contract help you pay to produce transcripts is a business expense, right at the top of the deduction list. If you 1099 a scopist, their pay is your cost. Track the payments as they happen, because reconstructing a year of Venmo sends in March is it's own special torment.

Mileage. Depositions, courthouses, the printer across town. The standard rate for 2026 was 72.5 cents a mile through June 30 and 76 cents from July 1, and business miles need a contemporaneous log, a spreadsheet updated weekly qualifies. A January memory exercise doesn't.

Equipment. The writer, the laptop, realtime cables, tripods for video depos. A steno machine can cross the de minimis threshold, so it's either Section 179 in the year you bought it or depreciated over time. Your tax software will ask. your receipts need to answer.

Software and subscriptions. CAT software licenses, realtime viewing seats, dictionary tools, the practice-management app that runs your billing. They recur, they're deductible, and they're easy to let lapse untracked. That streaming dictionary renewal never announces itself. One place to list them, updated when you add or drop a subscription.

Professional costs. Association dues, certification fees, CEU requirements, continuing seminars, the state license renewal. Professional maintenance is a deductible cost of staying in the trade.

Insurance and the home office. Errors-and-omissions coverage for your testimony work, plus the health and business policies you carry. And if you edit transcripts in a dedicated room, the home-office deduction applies to that space, measured honestly (the closet that stores the Christmas boxes does not count, no matter what the internet says).

Bank and payment fees. Card processing percentages, bank service charges, the wire fee your one Manhattan firm insisted on. Small individually, a four-figure category in aggregate for a busy reporter.

What to do each quarter

Sit down for forty minutes with the list and the banking app. Total income in. Totals out by category. Miles added to the log. Then pay the estimated tax that quarter's income suggests, on time and on the safe side. Four small meetings with yourself a year beat one large crisis every spring. This is also when the quarterly set-aside math happens, the set-aside calculator does it in a minute if the percentage habit hasn't stuck.

Where the money actually hides

The pattern that shows up in real reporter returns is the same three leaks. Mileage that went unbilled and therefore untracked. Scopist payments scattered across apps with no category attached, missed entirely. And subscriptions that quietly renewed at higher prices. None of these are exotic. All of them are pure margin, because the work was done and the tax was paid on income the deductions never offset.
